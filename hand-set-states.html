Set by hand

A valve turned by hand, a blind fitted, a hose moved, a padlock left off, a strip of tape. None of these sends a signal to anyone. Eleven investigation reports, read for one thing: who needed to know, and what they had.

Changes to this page

Published 6 October 2026. None since. Any change after publication is listed here with its date.

What this page is

A reading of eleven published investigation reports, from 1979 to 2025, for a single question. In each case something was set by hand and stayed as it was left. The person who made the next decision did not have that state in front of them, or had it and did not read it. The page quotes what each report says on that point and gives the link.

It is not an account of any of these accidents as a whole. Each report names many causes and the page takes up one thread. It assigns no blame and names no individual; companies are named as the reports name them. Many people died in these events; the reports were written so that it happens less often, and the page is offered in the same spirit.

Four ways it goes wrong

The state is real and cannot be seen from where the decision is made. The Herald, Martinez, Piper Alpha, Geismar. A ferry's bridge could not see the bow doors. A control room could not see a manual valve.

Something set for a test or for maintenance is never put back. La Porte, Buncefield, Three Mile Island, Aeroperú. The work was finished and handed over, and the state stayed as the work had left it.

The hand goes to the wrong thing. At Illiopolis it was the wrong reactor. At Martinez the procedure named one valve where there were two. At Pasadena the two hose connectors were identical. Knowing a position helps only if it is the position of the right object.

The state is shown and still not read. At Three Mile Island the lights were correct. On the Aeroperú aircraft the tape was there to be seen. A record prevents nothing by existing; someone has to read it, at a moment when it can still change the decision.

What each report says

Herald of Free Enterprise, off Zeebrugge

6 March 1987. The report counts not less than 150 passengers and 38 crew lost.

The ship sailed with her inner and outer bow doors open. The bridge had no indication of the doors. A sister ship had sailed with doors open in 1983, and in 1985 one of the company's masters asked for indicator lights:

"There is no indication on the bridge as to whether the most important watertight doors are closed or not." Report of Court No. 8074, section 18.4, quoting a master's memorandum of 28 June 1985

Marathon Martinez Renewables, California

19 November 2023. One person severely burned.

Two manual valves sat on the startup piping of a fired heater. One was at ground level. The other was elevated, hard to reach, and had no name of its own. An operator closed the first, by its label, as asked. The second stayed open, and part of the flow went round the heater downstream of the flow meter. The Board could not determine when that valve was opened.

"While personnel labeled the valves as "A" and "B" both in the field and on the operator control board, the new operating procedures still referred to only one "Taylor Bypass Valve."" U.S. Chemical Safety Board, final report
"The board operators could have noticed and corrected the valve misalignment, restoring flow to the heater well before the tube rupture, had the open-close status of these valves been visible to them." the same report, in a footnote

DuPont, La Porte, Texas

15 November 2014. Four people died.

During shutdown work in spring 2014 an interlock on a water valve was bypassed so that a tank could be washed. The bypass was still there months later. It was the first link in a long chain that the report sets out.

"Instrument air supply tubing was connected directly to the interlock valve actuator, bypassing the interlock activation solenoid, and forcing the valve to remain fully open. The interlock could not function because the bypass was not removed at the completion of the turnaround" U.S. Chemical Safety Board, report 2015-01-I-TX, in a footnote

Piper Alpha, North Sea

6 July 1988. 167 people died.

A relief valve had been removed from one of two condensate pumps and a blind flange fitted in its place, one floor above the pump. The permit for that work was suspended overnight and kept in a different office from the control room. When the other pump failed, the night shift moved to restart the first.

"Because of the way in which work permits were organised on Piper Alpha, the operators would not have known that the pressure relief valve for Pump A was missing." Engineers Ireland, summarising the Cullen inquiry. A secondary source; the inquiry report was not read.

Buncefield, England

11 December 2005. No deaths; over 40 people injured.

A storage tank had an independent high-level switch with a lever for testing it. The lever had to be held in its working position by a padlock. That was not passed on to those who fitted and maintained the switch. After tests the lever was left unsecured, and the switch could not act when the tank overfilled.

"The IHLS needed a padlock to retain its check lever in a working position." COMAH Competent Authority, "Buncefield: Why did it happen?"

Williams Olefins, Geismar, Louisiana

13 June 2013. Two people died.

Valves had been added that could cut a heat exchanger off from its pressure relief device. The safeguard chosen was a seal on the valve and a rule that it stays open. The standby exchanger sat with its valves closed, and heat was put into it.

"The CSB found that only the shell-side outlet valve of the operating reboiler was car sealed open." U.S. Chemical Safety Board, case study, October 2016

Formosa Plastics, Illiopolis, Illinois

23 April 2004. Five people died.

A reactor's bottom valve was held shut by an interlock while the reactor was under pressure. For emergencies there was a manual bypass: an air hose that could be moved straight onto the valve actuator. The report concludes that an operator cleaning one reactor likely went to another by mistake and used it.

"The emergency transfer procedure required a supervisor to authorize, but not necessarily witness, bypassing the interlock." U.S. Chemical Safety Board, report, March 2007, section 1.7.3

Three Mile Island unit 2, Pennsylvania

28 March 1979.

Two valves on the emergency feedwater lines were closed when the accident began. A routine test two days earlier had called for them to be closed for a time. It took about eight minutes for the operators to find them.

"The control panel lights correctly showed the valves closed at the time of the accident." President's Commission, Technical Staff Analysis Reports Summary, October 1979. Staff work, not the Commission's main report.
"there is no record kept of valve line-up" the same document

Phillips 66, Pasadena, Texas

23 October 1989. 23 people died.

A single ball valve stood between a reactor and a pipe that had been opened to clear a blockage. Witnesses said it was handed over closed, with its air hoses disconnected. It was open when the release came. The hoses were found connected in reverse, which opens the valve with its switch at closed.

"the air hose connectors for the "open" and "close" sides of the valve were identical" U.S. Department of Labor, OSHA, A Report to the President, April 1990

Aeroperú flight 603, off Lima

2 October 1996. 70 people on board.

Tape was put over the aircraft's static ports before the lower fuselage was polished, which the report calls normal practice. It was not taken off. The report lists the checks that followed and did not find it, and it places other findings ahead of the tape.

"no removió la cinta adhesiva protectora al concluir su trabajo, quedando así obturados los puertos estáticos." Peru, Dirección General de Transporte Aéreo, final report on N52AW, conclusions. In English: did not remove the protective tape on finishing the work, leaving the static ports blocked.

Cox Operating, South Marsh Island Block 99, Gulf coast of the United States

Late January 2023. An oil discharge; no injury reported.

Safety systems were placed in bypass for about three days with nobody monitoring them. The inspectors interviewed the staff and wrote that they "were unable to determine why". The summary does not say whether the bypass was made by hand at the equipment or at a panel, so this case may not belong in the list.

U.S. Department of the Interior, Office of Inspector General, summary 24-0090, 22 October 2025

One case left out, and why

Bhopal, December 1984, is often told as a missing blind: piping was washed without the plate that should have isolated it. Union Carbide's 1985 investigation report records that the blind was not used. It then says water entry by that route "would have required simultaneous leaks through several reportedly closed valves, which is highly improbable", and that "the exact source of the water is not known". That is one party's account and no other was read here. On this reading the case cannot be counted either way.

What this page does not establish

  • That any one of these states caused its accident. Each report names several causes.
  • That a record of the state would have prevented any of them. At Three Mile Island the indication was there and was not read.
  • Anything beyond the passages read. Each report was searched for the state set by hand and read around it. None was read from cover to cover.
  • The exact wording of three scanned reports (Herald, Pasadena, Bhopal), whose text is machine-read. Quotations from them were checked by eye against the surrounding lines.
  • Piper Alpha beyond a secondary summary, and Three Mile Island beyond a staff report.
  • Aeroperú beyond a text copy of the report held by a third party. The ministry's own file is a scan and the two were not compared.
