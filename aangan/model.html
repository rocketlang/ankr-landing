What this says

—

Because the house and farm staff already exist, the minimum path needs well under a crore and breaks even at just 6–7 of 10 beds — so its cash-flow hole is shallow and pays back fast. The realistic mid-build buys a resident doctor and a first floor without the ₹3.2 Cr bet. Either way the lesson holds: occupancy and reputation are the game, and the day club is the near-zero-capex way to build both — the verandah, drawing room and garden already are the venue. Prove demand there, then let these numbers size the beds.

Assumptions behind the model

  • Minimum — ₹0.50 Cr build (build up frugally on the existing single-floor house: 2 bedrooms, covered verandah and drawing room already stand) · ₹4.2 L/mo fixed (the farm's maid, driver and staff quarters already exist — you add one resident carer + 24/7 nursing) · ₹0.22 L/bed variable · default fee ₹1.00 L.
  • Realistic — ₹1.40 Cr build (upgrade + a frugal first floor on the existing base) · ₹7.5 L/mo fixed (resident doctor, a manager) · ₹0.28 L/bed variable · default fee ₹1.30 L.
  • Maximum — ₹3.20 Cr build (new luxury build) · ₹11.0 L/mo fixed · ₹0.35 L/bed variable · default fee ₹1.80 L.
  • Existing infrastructure is the biggest lever: because the house, the secured garden potential, and the farm's maid/driver/quarters are already in place, the minimum path avoids most of a normal build's capital and part of its fixed cost — which is why it pays back fast.
  • Ramp-up: beds fill roughly linearly from opening to mature occupancy over the months you set, then hold. Fixed costs run in full from day one (you staff to the care ratio immediately) — which is why the early months lose money.
  • Cash-flow = −(build) + running profit each month, no financing/interest, pre-tax. Round planning ranges for the Dhauj / NCR context; confirm with local quotes before committing capital.
